Conversational Recommender Systems with LLMs (2023–2025)
Introduction
Conversational Recommender Systems (CRS) enable users to interact through natural language dialogues to get personalized recommendations. Traditionally, building a CRS involved modular pipelines – separate components for candidate generation, ranking, and response generation – often using specialized models (e.g. GRU4Rec, SASRec for sequential recommendations or BERT-based rankers) trained on conversational data. Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-3/4, ChatGPT, and LLaMA have spurred a paradigm shift towards instruction-driven, unified approaches[1][2]. Instead of training task-specific models, we can prompt general-purpose LLMs with conversation context and let them generate recommendations and even conversational responses. This LLM-based approach leverages the models’ broad knowledge and reasoning ability, but also poses new challenges in evaluation, alignment, and prompt design.
This report surveys the latest research (2023–2025) on conversational recommenders and LLM-based re-ranking, highlighting key aspects: evaluation metrics, evaluation methods (offline vs. online), models (LLM-based and classical), and prompting strategies. We also list notable recent works (ACL, SIGIR, RecSys, WWW, etc., plus arXiv preprints) with their contributions.
Metrics for CRS Evaluation
Accuracy metrics: Like traditional recommenders, conversational recommenders are often evaluated with information-retrieval metrics. Common metrics include Recall/Hit Rate (how many relevant items are recommended), Precision, NDCG (rank-sensitive gain), MRR (reciprocal rank), and RMSE if predicting ratings[3]. For example, Zhu et al. (2025) report higher Recall and NDCG for their LLM-based approach compared to baselines[4]. In conversational settings, accuracy can be measured at multiple turns (e.g. success in recommending the desired item by the end of the dialogue).
Beyond-accuracy metrics: It has long been recognized that accuracy alone doesn’t ensure user satisfaction[5]. Key complementary metrics include diversity (how varied the recommendations are) and novelty (how new/unseen items are to the user)[6]. Recent LLM-based studies explicitly optimize diversity. Carraro & Bridge (2024) measure genre diversity of movie recommendations and use classical diversity metrics (e.g. intra-list diversity) to compare LLM re-rankers with methods like MMR or xQuAD[7][8]. Other works also consider serendipity (surprise) and fairness, especially in multi-objective recommendation settings[9][10]. A survey by Raja et al. (2025) notes that prompts can be engineered to target these objectives (e.g. “recommend diverse items” or “include underrepresented items”)[11][12].
Conversational quality metrics: A CRS must produce not only good item suggestions but also coherent dialogue. Traditional NLP metrics like BLEU, ROUGE, or perplexity are used to evaluate the language quality of responses[3]. For example, if the system explains or chats, these metrics gauge fluency and relevance of the utterances. However, recent research cautions that such automatic metrics correlate weakly with user perceptions[13]. Manzoor et al. (2024) found that human-rated “meaningfulness” of ChatGPT’s responses had only a very weak correlation with BLEU/ROUGE scores[14]. This underlines the importance of human evaluation for dialogue quality.
User-centric metrics: In interactive recommender evaluation, user satisfaction is paramount. Some studies use composite measures – e.g. Manzoor et al. define “perceived meaningfulness” of a response, capturing both recommendation relevance and conversational coherence as judged by users[15]. Others look at engagement or response helpfulness via user surveys. Explainable recommendation research also evaluates explanation quality, sometimes via human annotators or crowdworkers. Wang et al. (2023) emphasize evaluating explainability in LLM-based CRS, noting that ChatGPT can generate persuasive explanations for its recommendations[16].
Evaluation Methods: Offline vs. Online
Offline evaluation on benchmark datasets remains the primary method for research experiments. Common CRS datasets include ReDial (movie dialogues with recommendations), INSPIRED (conversational music recs), OpenDialKG (dialogues grounded in a knowledge graph), among others. Models are typically evaluated by how well they predict the “ground-truth” items in these test dialogs. For example, Li et al. (2025) report accuracy gains on ReDial and INSPIRED datasets to show the effectiveness of their approach[17]. He et al. (2023) introduce a new large dataset of real-world convos scraped from the web and show LLMs outperform prior models on both this and existing datasets[18].
However, static offline metrics can be misleading for conversational systems. Wang et al. (2023) argue that the prevailing protocol – which often checks if the recommended item matches a single ground-truth item – overemphasizes one-off matching and neglects the interactive nature of conversation[19]. A conversation might have multiple acceptable recommendations, or a user may be satisfied even if the exact ground-truth item isn’t recommended, as long as the dialogue is helpful. To address this, Wang et al. propose iEvaLM, an interactive evaluation with an LLM-based user simulator, so the CRS is tested in a simulated dialog loop[16]. By simulating how a user might respond to recommendations and allowing multi-turn interactions, they obtain a more realistic evaluation of dialogue success. They demonstrate that this approach yields insights beyond static metrics and can evaluate how the CRS adapts over a conversation[16].
Beyond simulations, researchers are exploring user simulators extensively. For example, Zhu et al. (2025b) develop a controllable, human-involved user simulator (CSHI) to generate realistic conversations[20]. Their simulator allows injecting profiles or goals and even has plugin modules to mimic memory and preference shifts, making the simulated interaction more lifelike (e.g. distinguishing what preferences the user explicitly knows vs. discovers during the chat)[21][22]. Such simulators can not only evaluate CRS in a controlled way but also generate new training dialogs, which may help address data scarcity[23][24].
Online evaluation (with real users) is the gold standard for CRS. This can be through A/B tests in a live system or controlled user studies. Due to the cost, few academic works include live A/B tests, but Manzoor et al. (2024) conducted a randomized user study with 190 participants to compare ChatGPT (as a CRS) against a strong retrieval-based baseline[25]. In their study, users interacted with one of the systems over multiple dialog scenarios and rated the responses. The results showed ChatGPT’s responses were perceived as significantly more meaningful than the baseline’s[15]. Interestingly, they observed ChatGPT excelled at providing recommendations (users liked its suggestions), but it sometimes lost context on follow-up questions in longer dialogs[15]. They also statistically analyzed which factors in responses impact user satisfaction – finding that information adequacy and recommendation accuracy of the reply most strongly influenced the user’s perceived quality[26]. Such user studies complement offline metrics by revealing if improvements translate to better user experience. They also highlighted mismatches between automatic metrics and human opinion, reinforcing that human-in-the-loop evaluation is crucial[13].
In summary, the community is moving toward more robust evaluation for conversational recommenders: using interactive simulations (LLM-based users), multi-faceted metrics (beyond one-shot accuracy), and direct user feedback when possible.
Models and Approaches
Classical CRS models: Before LLMs, conv recommender models often combined a recommender backbone with a dialogue module. For example, knowledge-grounded CRS like KBRD or KGSF used graph-based recommenders plus an NLG component. Sequential recommendation models (GRU4Rec, SASRec, etc.) have been adapted to conversation by treating mentioned items and attributes in the dialogue as a sequence input to predict next items[27]. These models rely on entity embeddings, collaborative filtering signals, and supervised training on dialog datasets. They typically require training on domain-specific data and struggle with understanding rich language outside their training. Nevertheless, such models (or their components) remain useful – e.g. Li et al. (2025) use a lightweight transformer recommender to process the dialogue’s entity sequence and generate candidate items, which the LLM will later re-rank[28][29]. Using a classical model as a “domain expert” ensures coverage of the catalog items and captures collaborative patterns that an LLM might miss in zero-shot settings[30][31]. In fact, He et al. (2023) noted that ChatGPT, without adaptation, often recommends items outside the target domain or popular items due to its training data bias[30][31]. This has motivated hybrid systems that bridge LLMs with traditional recommenders.
LLM-based methods: The simplest approach treats the LLM as a black-box conversational agent. For example, you prompt ChatGPT with the conversation so far and ask for the next recommendation or response. He et al. (2023) showed that such zero-shot LLM recommenders can surprisingly outperform fine-tuned conventional CRS models on some datasets[18]. LLMs leverage their immense knowledge (e.g. about movies, books, etc.) and language understanding to interpret user preferences from dialogue context. However, challenges include: (1) catalog awareness – LLMs might recommend items that aren’t in the platform’s database (out-of-catalog)[32][33], (2) no explicit training on recommendation objective – they might not rank optimally for user utility, and (3) format and consistency – vanilla LLMs may not follow required output format or may give inconsistent results in a list (e.g. the last few items in a generated top-10 list might be irrelevant[34][35]). Recent research addresses these limitations with adaptation and fine-tuning.
One insight is to represent items in natural language (titles, attributes) when using LLMs, rather than embedding opaque item IDs. This preserves the LLM’s language understanding and allows incorporating objectives like diversity or novelty directly via prompts[36]. For example, Rank-GRPO (Zhu et al. 2025a) avoids feeding raw item IDs to the model; instead it uses descriptive item metadata so the LLM can reason about items and be guided to prefer certain properties[37]. The authors note this enables flexible objectives (e.g. “recommend something different from what the user already mentioned”) to be imposed via language instructions, which is hard with ID-based models[37].
Fine-tuning and RL: Several works go beyond prompting and actually fine-tune LLMs for recommendation tasks, while still leveraging their pre-trained knowledge. Instruction tuning is popular: Luo et al. (2024) in RecRanker created a custom instruction dataset for ranking tasks and fine-tuned a LLaMA-2 model to act as a ranker[38][39]. They incorporated traditional signals (e.g. features from collaborative filtering models) into the prompts during training, to “teach” the LLM domain-specific knowledge it lacks[38][40]. This approach resulted in a tuned model that consistently outperforms both conventional recommenders and zero-shot LLMs on top-K recommendation benchmarks, demonstrating over 70% improvement in hit rate on a book recommendation dataset (BookCrossing) in their experiments. Similarly, Wen et al. (2025) in USB-Rec use reinforcement learning (RL) to train an LLM-based CRS policy[41]. They generate a synthetic training corpus of conversations using a user simulator (to provide the LLM with feedback on good vs. bad recommendation strategies) and perform RL fine-tuning (a form of RLHF – reinforcement learning with human/simulated feedback)[41][42]. The RL-trained model, combined with a self-enhancement inference strategy (sampling multiple response candidates and voting), achieved state-of-the-art results on ReDial and OpenDialKG[43][44] – indicating that training the LLM on conversational recommendation patterns yields substantial gains over zero-shot usage.
Another advanced technique is policy optimization for ranking. Zhu et al. (2025a) propose Rank-GRPO, a variant of RL fine-tuning that treats each position in the recommendation list as a step and optimizes the LLM to place high-value items in top ranks[45][46]. By redefining the reward at the rank-level (rather than just sequence-level), they tackle the credit assignment problem where an overall list’s NDCG is hard for the model to improve. Their two-stage training (first using a distilled set of high-quality demonstrations, then RL fine-tuning with Rank-GRPO) led to faster convergence and higher final recalls/NDCG than standard RL approaches[4]. These fine-tuning approaches blur the line between “LLM” and “classical model” – essentially creating a specialized recommendation-focused LLM, often by injecting knowledge from classical recommenders during training.
Hybrid systems: A promising direction is combining LLM reasoning with recommender precision. The CARE framework (Li et al., 2025) is one example: it runs a domain-specific recommender (a transformer sequence model) on the conversation’s entities to get an initial candidate list, then prompts the LLM with those candidates plus the dialogue context to generate the final recommendations[28][29]. The LLM here plays the role of a contextual re-ranker, ensuring the final list fits the nuanced user preferences expressed in conversation. This modular approach yielded dramatic accuracy improvements – CARE reports a 54% and 25% increase in top-5 accuracy on ReDial and INSPIRED respectively, compared to prior LLM-only methods[17]. Notably, the best performing variant was the one where the LLM selects and re-ranks the candidates from the external model using the conversation as guidance[47]. This shows that LLMs can effectively inject conversational context (including subtle cues like user sentiment or negations) on top of a strong candidate set from a traditional recommender. The hybrid approach also mitigates popularity bias – the external model may over-recommend popular items, but the LLM can override some of those with contextually relevant, possibly less popular items[48].
In summary, recent models span a spectrum: from zero-shot LLM agents to fully fine-tuned LLM recommenders, with hybrid strategies leveraging the best of classic and LLM paradigms. Early results are promising: LLMs bring flexibility (handling diverse queries, providing explanations) and rich reasoning, while careful integration or fine-tuning addresses their blind spots (catalog limitations, ranking finesse).
Prompt Design in LLM-based Recommenders
Since LLMs follow prompts, how we formulate the prompt is crucial for good recommendations. Recent works have explored various prompt engineering techniques:
Zero-shot instructional prompts: These provide the task description and context to the LLM with no examples. For instance, to prompt an LLM to re-rank for diversity, Carraro & Bridge gave instructions like “Reorder the following list of movies to maximize genre diversity while staying relevant to the user’s interest” (paraphrased) and found this more effective than asking for a balance of diversity and relevance[49]. Zero-shot prompts can also set role and style (e.g. “You are a movie recommendation assistant.”).
Few-shot prompting: Here, the prompt includes one or more example dialogues with ideal recommendations, before the actual user query. This can prime the LLM to follow a certain format or strategy. For instance, a prompt might show: User: “I loved The Matrix, any suggestions?” – Assistant: “Sure! You might enjoy Inception because… (plus explanation)”, and then the new conversation context, expecting the model to continue in that style. Few-shot examples can teach the LLM to include explanations or diversify results as needed[50].
Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting: This involves asking the LLM to reason step-by-step. In a recommendation scenario, a CoT prompt could instruct the model to first list the user’s stated preferences, then list candidate items and reasons, and finally output a recommendation. Raja et al. (2025) note that CoT prompting is an emerging tactic to improve LLM reasoning for recommendation ranking[50]. CoT can help the model consider multiple factors (e.g. “The user mentioned liking sci-fi and also enjoyed a recent thriller; perhaps I should recommend a movie that blends those genres…” internally before giving an answer).
Structured prompt templates: Some research creates systematic templates. For example, in the diversity re-ranking study, a prompt template was defined with three sections: (1) an instruction header (explaining the goal like “maximize diversity” and any constraints), (2) a required output format (e.g. “Output the reordered list of item IDs in JSON array”), and (3) the data context (the candidate list and possibly user profile)[51][52]. Using consistent templates helps maintain output reliability. Carraro & Bridge experimented with multiple template variants (some emphasizing diversity, some balancing it with relevance) to see which yields the best trade-off[53][54].
Prompt tuning and soft prompts: Beyond textual engineering, some works (e.g. RecRanker) effectively “learn” prompts by optimizing prompt augmentations during fine-tuning[38]. Prompt enhancement in RecRanker involved adding auxiliary info (like important features from a collaborative model) into the prompt to guide the LLM[40]. There are also approaches of learning continuous prompt embeddings (soft prompts), though in recommender literature this is less explored so far.
Designing prompts for multi-objective goals is especially challenging. Raja et al.’s survey (2025) highlights that expressing concepts like novelty or fairness in a prompt can be ambiguous[11]. Researchers have tried phrases like “recommend something the user hasn’t seen before” for novelty, or “include a mix of popular and niche items” for diversity. The survey emphasizes the need for systematic evaluation of how well different prompt wordings actually achieve the intended objective[55]. It also notes open issues: prompts can be sensitive to phrasing and there is no standard benchmark yet to compare prompt strategies on fairness/diversity trade-offs[55].
Examples of prompts: In published works, we often find examples in appendices. Carraro & Bridge (2024) provide sample ChatGPT prompts used in their study[56]. One example from their Appendix (summarized) shows the prompt asking: “Here is a list of 20 anime movies [titles] recommended based on a user’s preference. Please reorder these to make the list as diverse as possible in terms of genre, while still relevant to what the user likes.” The output was expected as a re-ranked list. They also asked comparison questions like “Which of these two lists is more diverse?” to test LLMs’ understanding of diversity[57]. In the CARE framework, the prompt given to the LLM included both the dialogue history and a set of candidate item names, with instructions to “select from these candidates the best items for the user now” (implicitly incorporating contextual cues like the user’s last utterance)[31][29]. This guided the LLM to not hallucinate new items but choose from provided ones in a context-aware way.
Another interesting prompt design is in explainable CRS. Some works prompt LLMs to generate a recommendation along with an explanation. For example: “Recommend a book for the user and explain why you think they would like it based on the conversation.” Human evaluations by Guo et al. (2023) indicated that ChatGPT could produce convincing explanations, and users appreciated them[58]. Prompt-wise, this is simply appending “and explain your reasoning” to the request, but the challenge is ensuring factual and relevant explanations.
In conclusion, prompt engineering for conversational recommenders is evolving into a fine art, combining domain knowledge, user intent, and NLP best practices. Effective prompts turn LLMs into powerful recommenders without heavy re-training, but they require careful crafting and sometimes task-specific tuning. As LLM-based recommenders mature, we can expect more standardized prompt collections and possibly automated prompt optimization for different recommendation goals.

Notable Recent Works (2023–2025) in Conversational Recommendation
Below we list several influential papers and preprints from 2023–2025, along with their key contributions, focusing on conversational recommenders and LLM-based re-ranking. Each work is from a reputable venue or arXiv and helps illustrate the trends discussed above.
Large Language Models as Zero-Shot Conversational Recommenders (2023) – Zhankui He, Zhouhang Xie, Rahul Jha, Harald Steck, Dawen Liang, Yesu Feng, B. P. Majumder, Nathan Kallus, Julian McAuley. DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2308.10053. Summary: Pioneering study demonstrating that general LLMs (like ChatGPT) can serve as conversational recommenders without additional training. The authors introduce a new “in-the-wild” CRS dataset scraped from online discussions and evaluate LLMs in a zero-shot setting[59]. Remarkably, even without fine-tuning, ChatGPT outperformed several specialized CRS models on this and two existing datasets[18]. They also analyze why: LLMs have broad knowledge and language understanding, enabling them to infer preferences and make relevant suggestions. The paper provides probing tasks to understand LLM behavior and discusses LLMs’ limitations (e.g. occasional irrelevant or out-of-domain recommendations) and future directions[60].
Rethinking the Evaluation for Conversational Recommendation in the Era of Large Language Models (2023) – Xiaolei Wang, Xinyu Tang, Xin Zhao, Jingyuan Wang, Ji-Rong Wen. DOI: 10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.621. Summary: This EMNLP 2023 paper argues that traditional evaluation protocols for CRS are inadequate when applied to LLM-based systems[19]. The authors point out that current offline metrics overly focus on matching a single “ground-truth” item and fail to capture the interactive and multi-turn aspects of conversation[19]. They propose iEvaLM, an interactive LLM-driven evaluation method, where an LLM acts as a user simulator to converse with the recommender[16]. By simulating diverse user behaviors and dialogue paths, iEvaLM evaluates how well the system adapts and satisfies the user over a conversation (not just one turn). Experiments on two public CRS datasets showed this method can reveal improvements that static metrics miss[16]. They also emphasize evaluating explainability – interestingly, ChatGPT as a recommender could generate persuasive natural-language explanations for its recommendations, adding value beyond the recommendation accuracy[58]. This work encourages a shift towards more holistic CRS evaluation in the LLM era.
ChatGPT as a Conversational Recommender System: A User-Centric Analysis (2024) – Ahtsham Manzoor, Samuel C. Ziegler, Klaus M. Pirker Garcia, Dietmar Jannach. DOI: 10.1145/3627043.3659574. Summary: A controlled user study (published at UMAP 2024) investigating how users perceive ChatGPT’s recommendations. The authors had 190 participants interact with ChatGPT (as a movie recommender) and with a baseline retrieval-based CRS in multiple dialog scenarios[25]. Users overwhelmingly preferred ChatGPT’s responses, finding them significantly more meaningful and helpful than the baseline’s[15]. ChatGPT especially excelled at making recommendations that users liked. However, the study noted a weakness: in longer conversations, ChatGPT sometimes forgot or missed context when answering follow-up questions about a previously recommended item[15]. Through statistical analysis, the paper finds that a response’s information adequacy and recommendation relevance were the strongest predictors of user satisfaction (more so than its linguistic fluency)[26]. Another key finding is the discrepancy between automatic metrics and human judgment – for example, BLEU/ROUGE scores had only a weak correlation with user ratings of the responses[13]. The authors conclude that while LLMs like ChatGPT are a big step forward for conversational recommenders (achieving high user satisfaction), careful consideration is needed in evaluating them, and there’s room to improve how they maintain context over long dialogues.
Enhancing Recommendation Diversity by Re-ranking with Large Language Models (2024) – Diego Carraro, Derek Bridge. DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2401.11506 (also in ACM RecSys Transactions, 2024). Summary: This work investigates using LLMs to improve the diversity of recommendations via reranking. The authors note that beyond accuracy, offering a diverse list (covering different genres or categories) can increase user satisfaction[5]. They conduct experiments where an initial list of recommendations (generated by a standard model like matrix factorization) is fed into prompts for GPT-family and Llama-2 models, asking the LLM to produce a “more diverse” ordering[61][62]. They compare LLM-based reranking against classic diversification algorithms like MMR and xQuAD. Findings: LLMs do understand diversity to an extent – the LLM-based re-ranker consistently beat a random reranking baseline in all diversity and relevance metrics[62]. However, LLM reranking still fell short of specialized methods like MMR in achieving the best diversity-relevance trade-off[62]. The authors also experimented with prompt phrasing: interestingly, they found that explicitly prompting the LLM to “maximize diversity” yielded better results than asking it to “balance diversity and relevance”[49]. This suggests LLMs might struggle with multi-objective instructions and perform better when the prompt focus is clearer. Despite not yet topping traditional methods, the approach is deemed promising, especially given that it requires no additional training or domain knowledge – an LLM can be plugged in to re-rank on the fly[63]. The paper opens avenues for using LLMs to optimize other objectives (novelty, fairness) by prompt engineering.
CARE: Contextual Adaptation of Recommenders for LLM-based Conversational Recommendation (2025) – Chuang Li, Yang Deng, Hengchang Hu, See-Kiong Ng, Min-Yen Kan, Haizhou Li. DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2508.13889. Summary: CARE is a two-stage framework that combines the strengths of traditional recommenders and LLMs for conversational recommendation. The paper identifies two major challenges for zero-shot LLM recommenders: (1) Item space discrepancy – LLMs often suggest items outside the platform’s catalog or domain[32][33], and (2) Neglect of collaborative signals – LLMs rely mostly on content of the conversation and may ignore patterns in user–item interactions (which a collab-filtering model would catch)[30][31]. CARE addresses these by first using an external recommender (a lightweight transformer-based sequential model) to generate candidate items from the conversation’s entities[28]. This external model acts as a domain expert, ensuring candidates are valid and leveraging any available structured knowledge (e.g. past interactions, knowledge graph)[30][31]. In the second stage, an LLM is prompted with the dialogue context + the candidate list to output the final recommendations, effectively re-ranking or selecting candidates using rich contextual understanding[31][29]. CARE showed large gains in accuracy over both pure-training-based baselines and pure-LLM baselines: for example, incorporating the external recommender boosted LLM recommender accuracy by ~54% on ReDial and ~25% on INSPIRED[17]. The best variant had the LLM select and rank the provided candidates (as opposed to, say, just generate new items)[47]. By injecting dialogue nuances (like the user’s stated intent or sentiments) into the reranking step, CARE produces more relevant and personalized results. They also report that CARE mitigates popularity bias – the external model might propose popular items, but the LLM can down-rank those if they don’t fit the user’s specific request[48]. This work is a strong proof-of-concept for LLM+Recommender collaboration, showing that we don’t have to choose between them when building CRS.
Rank-GRPO: Training LLM-based Conversational Recommender Systems with Reinforcement Learning (2025) – Yaochen Zhu, Harald Steck, Dawen Liang, Yinhan He, Vito Ostuni, Jundong Li, Nathan Kallus. DOI: arXiv:2510.20150. Summary: This research (from Netflix & UVA) tackles the problem of aligning LLMs to the recommendation task using reinforcement learning. The authors note that out-of-the-box LLMs often generate non-optimal recommendation lists: e.g. they may include items not in the catalog, fail to follow the required format, or put less relevant items toward the end of a long list[34][64]. Rank-GRPO introduces a solution in two stages: Stage 1: Behavioral Cloning – they generate high-quality demonstration data using a novel Remap–Reflect–Adjust pipeline (essentially using a powerful black-box LLM to create example conversations with grounded, well-ranked recommendations) to warm-start the policy[65]. Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning – they propose Rank-GRPO, a variant of the GRPO algorithm, which treats each rank position as a step and optimizes a reward like NDCG in a rank-wise manner[45][66]. This avoids the pitfall of standard policy gradient that would give one big reward for a whole list (which is hard to attribute to individual item choices)[66]. In experiments on a Reddit-based conversational recommendation dataset, their ConvRec-R1 method (the two-stage training with Rank-GRPO) converged faster and achieved higher Recall@k and NDCG@k than baseline methods including supervised fine-tuning and vanilla GRPO[4]. The trained LLM was better at keeping relevant items at top ranks. Importantly, the model learns to avoid out-of-catalog items and maintain output consistency by this training. This work is notable for injecting ranking optimization directly into LLM training, pointing toward LLMs that can inherently act as recommendation rankers.
USB-Rec: An Effective Framework for Improving Conversational Recommendation Capability of Large Language Model (2025) – Jianyu Wen, Jingyun Wang, Cilin Yan, Jiayin Cai, Xiaolong Jiang, Ying Zhang. DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2509.20381 (Accepted at ACM RecSys 2025). Summary: USB-Rec proposes a training + inference framework to boost an LLM’s conversational recommendation performance by addressing the lack of direct training signal in prior approaches. The “USB” stands for User-Simulator-Based framework. During training, they create a Preference Optimization (PO) dataset by having an LLM-based user simulator interact with the recommender LLM[41]. Essentially, the simulator provides conversational feedback that helps the LLM learn good recommendation strategies (this is somewhat akin to RLHF but specific to CRS). They then apply reinforcement learning (RL) on this data, optimizing the LLM to choose items that would satisfy the simulated user’s preference. At inference time, USB-Rec introduces a Self-Enhancement Strategy (SES)[41]: the LLM generates multiple possible recommendations (by sampling with slight randomness) and uses a voting or re-scoring mechanism to select the final output, which leverages the idea that an ensemble of its own generations can improve reliability[42]. This two-pronged approach (improve the base model via RL, then refine outputs via self-ensemble) led to state-of-the-art results. The paper reports consistent outperformance of previous SOTA on several datasets[43]. For example, on the movie dialogue dataset ReDial and the multi-domain OpenDialKG, USB-Rec’s recommendations achieved higher accuracy than baseline LLM-based methods and even prior specialized models[43][44]. This indicates that training LLMs with simulated conversational feedback (to align their behavior) is a powerful tool, and that even after training, leveraging the LLM’s own generative diversity (via SES voting) can further boost performance. USB-Rec is an important step toward making LLMs actively learn to be better recommenders, rather than using them in zero-shot or with passive fine-tuning only.
RecRanker: Instruction Tuning Large Language Model as Ranker for Top-k Recommendation (2024) – Sichun Luo, Bowei He, Haohan Zhao, Wei Shao, Yanlin Qi, Yinya Huang, Aojun Zhou, Yuxuan Yao, Zongpeng Li, Yuanzhang Xiao, Mingjie Zhan, Linqi Song. DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2312.16018 (to appear in ACM TOIS 2025). Summary: RecRanker is an approach to directly fine-tune an LLM to act as a recommendation ranker. Unlike zero-shot prompting, here the LLM is trained (instruction-tuned) on a variety of ranking tasks so that it can take in a set of candidate items and output a properly ordered list. The authors address two key issues: the need for high-quality training data and the need to incorporate traditional recommender signals (since pure LLM training data may not include, say, user-item interaction nuances)[67][68]. They introduce an adaptive sampling module to curate a training dataset that is representative and diverse – sampling user interactions and items in a way that covers different scenarios and difficulty levels[69]. They also propose a position shifting strategy to augment prompts during training, which combats position bias (so the model doesn’t learn to always put certain item types at a specific rank)[38]. Moreover, RecRanker’s prompts are enhanced with auxiliary info from conventional models – for example, the prompt given to the LLM during training might include each candidate item’s score from a matrix factorization model, or some collaborative filtering features[40]. This way, the LLM isn’t ranking from scratch but is informed by proven signals. They train the model on three kinds of instruction tasks: pointwise (rate a single item), pairwise (choose which item is better for the user), and listwise (rank a set of items)[70]. A final hybrid ranking approach is used at inference, ensembling the model’s outputs to leverage what it learned from all three task types[71]. Empirical results show RecRanker outperforms various baselines in both standard (static) top-K recommendation and a sequential scenario[72]. Notably, it beat not only a zero-shot ChatGPT but also strong non-LLM models on benchmarks like MovieLens and BookCrossing, with substantial improvements reported (e.g. significant Hit@5 and NDCG@5 gains). This work illustrates that with carefully designed training data and objectives, an LLM can be transformed into a highly effective learning-to-rank model for recommendations[72]. It underscores the benefit of marrying LLMs with traditional recommender knowledge through instruction tuning.
Aligning Prompts with Ranking Goals: A Technical Review of Prompt Engineering for LLM-Based Recommendations (2025) – Rahul Raja, Arpita Vats, Sudipta Roy. DOI: 10.20944/preprints202509.1959.v1. Summary: This is a comprehensive survey (preprint) focusing on prompt engineering techniques in recommender systems that use LLMs. It recognizes that while there’s burgeoning interest in LLM-based recommenders, there’s a lack of systematic understanding of how to craft prompts to achieve specific objectives (beyond just relevance)[73]. The review proposes a taxonomy of prompts used in recommendation scenarios: from zero-shot prompts with plain instructions, to few-shot prompts with demonstrations, to chain-of-thought prompts that elicit step-by-step reasoning[73]. It categorizes prompt usage by stage of recommendation (e.g. prompting for candidate generation vs. prompting for reranking vs. prompting for explanation)[9][12]. A central theme is how to align prompts with multi-objective ranking goals like diversity, novelty, serendipity, and fairness[11]. The survey discusses example prompt templates targeting each of these goals and reviews what recent empirical studies have found – for instance, the difficulty of prompting for “fair” recommendations without inducing unwanted side-effects. It also compares static prompting vs. learned prompts vs. fine-tuning approaches, weighing their pros and cons[50]. Important challenges highlighted include: prompt sensitivity (small wording changes can flip outcomes), robustness (prompts that work across different LLMs or domains), and the lack of standardized evaluation benchmarks for prompts under multi-objective criteria[55]. The paper concludes with suggested directions, such as developing a unified evaluation framework for prompt effectiveness and exploring human-in-the-loop prompt refinement[55]. For practitioners and researchers, this survey serves as a roadmap of how to leverage prompt engineering to coax the best recommendation performance from LLMs, while cautioning that we need better evaluation practices to truly measure success in those broader objectives.
A LLM-based Controllable, Scalable, Human-Involved User Simulator Framework for Conversational Recommender Systems (2025) – Lixi Zhu, Xiaowen Huang, Jitao Sang, et al. DOI: 10.1145/3696410.3714858 (The Web Conference WWW 2025). Summary: This work addresses the need for advanced user simulators in evaluating and training conversational recommenders. It presents a framework called CSHI (Controllable, Scalable, Human-Involved) built on LLMs to simulate users in CRS dialogues. Key features of CSHI include the ability to set and edit user profiles and preferences (even allowing human experts to tweak a simulated user’s persona or goals)[74], and a modular design with plugins that handle different aspects of user behavior. For example, there’s a plugin for simulating long-term preferences (based on a profile or past interactions) and another for real-time preference shifts during the conversation[21][22]. The simulator can thus mimic realistic behaviors like a user initially asking for a movie recommendation, then refining their request when given options, or expressing new preferences when the conversation drifts. The human-involved aspect means developers can inject specific scenarios or even have a human take over the simulated user mid-dialogue for hybrid testing. To avoid trivializing the task, the simulator is designed to prevent data leakage – e.g. it won’t reveal the target item outright, and it differentiates between what the user knows and what they discover through the conversation[75][22]. The authors demonstrate CSHI in two CRS scenarios (one movie-focused using OpenDialKG data, and another multi-domain) and show that it can adapt to various system strategies and provide informative feedback for evaluation[23]. They also mention using this simulator to generate new conversational data, which could be used to train or fine-tune CRS models[24]. A web-based demo allowed real users to interact with the simulator, validating that its behavior felt plausible. Overall, this framework represents a significant step towards scalable and flexible CRS evaluation, enabling rigorous testing of conversational recommenders in simulation before deploying to real users. By incorporating LLMs, the simulator can produce natural language interactions that are far more complex and realistic than rule-based simulators of the past, while the control features ensure researchers can target specific conditions (cold-start users, specific preference quirks, etc.) in their experiments.
